# LeetCode Premium: what the paid tiers actually unlock, when the yearly plan pays off, and the cheaper pattern-first route

Most people who search for LeetCode Premium aren't looking for a feature list. They've hit a wall — a recruiter email, an online assessment in nine days, or three weeks of grinding mediums with nothing sticking — and they want to know one thing: is the paywall going to fix it?

So let's answer that properly. First the prices as they stand, then what the money actually buys, then the part most reviews skip: the bottleneck Premium doesn't touch, and what a cheaper structured course like AlgoMonster does differently.

## Current LeetCode Premium prices, and why yours may differ

LeetCode's own subscription page currently shows two plans:

- **Monthly: $39/month**, billed monthly
- **Yearly: $179/year**, billed once (the page marks it down from a former $299 list price, and prices it out at roughly $14.92/month — a saving it claims is over 62% versus monthly)

That's what the page serves in the default US view. You'll also run into 2026 write-ups quoting $35/month and $159/year, which is what the page showed at other points. And LeetCode prices by region: at least one reviewer loading the page from an Indian IP was shown ₹999/month and ₹6,999/year, with the plans labelled as available in India only.

The practical takeaway is boring but useful: the number in your browser is the only number that applies to you, and it can shift. Don't budget off a blog post, including this one.

There's also a student path that most people miss. LeetCode's Back to School 2026 promotion, announced in mid-August, offers Premium at **$99** for students when 50 or more students from the same school join the event by September 30, 2026. That's roughly half the standard annual price if your campus can organise itself — and the event also carries a Campus Ambassador track with rewards. It's a group-dependent deal, so it's worth checking whether your school already has people in it before you pay full freight.

## What Premium adds that the free tier withholds

The free LeetCode account is more generous than people expect: the bulk of the problem library, unlimited submissions with the real judge, weekly and biweekly contests, the discussion forums with community solutions, and progress tracking all cost nothing. Plenty of candidates pass serious interviews on it.

Premium buys three things on top of that.

**Company-tagged question lists.** This is the feature the overwhelming majority of subscribers are actually paying for — filtering problems by the company you're interviewing with. The tag filters are Premium-only; there's no free workaround beyond browsing community threads and hoping someone mentions the pattern.

**Premium-locked problems and official editorials.** A minority of problems carry the Premium lock, and while community solutions are often excellent, some official editorial content sits behind the paywall.

**Interview tooling.** LeetCode's subscription page lists Ask Leet, its coding agent, with 500 extra monthly credits for Premium members. There's also the interview-simulation side of the platform, which is more convenience than capability.

Notice what isn't on that list: teaching. Premium doesn't explain why a sliding window fits a problem and a two-pointer pass doesn't. It gives you a better target list and a better editor, then assumes you already know how to convert "this is a sliding window problem" into working code under a clock.

## The test that decides whether Premium is worth it

Run yourself through this before you enter a card number.

You're a good candidate for the yearly plan if you can already solve mediums with reasonable consistency, you're targeting specific named companies, and your prep window is two months or longer. At that point Premium is a targeting tool aimed at a skill you already have, and $179 against a salary negotiation is a rounding error.

Buy a single month instead if your interviews are actually scheduled and close. One $39 month, spent entirely on company tags for the companies in your pipeline, is the efficient version of this purchase. There's no reason to hold a subscription through months when you're not interviewing.

Buy nothing if you open a medium problem and don't recognise which data structure is even in play. That's not a Premium problem. That's a "you haven't learned the patterns yet" problem, and a company-tag filter will just route you to harder versions of questions you can't solve. The yearly plan is the most commonly regretted $179 in interview prep, and it's almost always bought at exactly this stage.

Worth stating plainly: paying for Premium doesn't transfer discipline, and it doesn't compress a learning curve into a subscription.

## The bottleneck Premium doesn't fix: choosing the pattern

Here's the failure mode that shows up over and over in interview debriefs. A candidate can write clean code. They know what a heap is. They sit down with a problem they haven't seen, spend fifteen of the forty-five minutes trying approaches that don't fit the input structure, and then rush an implementation. The interviewer writes "needed hints on approach" and that's the round.

That gap — recognising which of roughly 25 to 50 recurring patterns a fresh problem belongs to — is what's actually being tested in a coding screen. LeetCode's model assumes you'll absorb it by volume: solve enough problems and the recognition shows up. That works. It also takes months, and it's a genuinely bad fit if you have a six-week runway and a day job.

The alternative model is to learn the patterns explicitly first, then use problem volume to build speed. That's the niche AlgoMonster occupies.

Two design choices matter more than the feature list. First, **there is no video**. Everything is text and diagrams, which makes it fast to skim and searchable when you're revising the night before. Second, **the curriculum is built to be finished in four to eight weeks** at an hour or two a day. That endpoint is the actual product. LeetCode's 3,000-plus problems have no finish line; 48 patterns do.

The honest limits are worth knowing before you buy. 231 problems is not enough volume for interviews where fluency under pressure is the differentiator — most working engineers treat AlgoMonster as the framework and LeetCode as the gym. System design coverage is introductory, fine for junior loops and not enough for L5-and-above rounds. Behavioural prep is thin. There's no live mock interview with a real person, which is the one gap no self-paced platform closes.

## FAQ

**Is LeetCode Premium required to pass a FAANG interview?** No. The free tier covers the majority of the problem bank, contests and discussions, and plenty of candidates pass on curated free lists plus community solutions. Premium's genuinely unique asset is company-tagged questions, and that only matters when you're targeting specific employers.

**How much does LeetCode Premium cost?** LeetCode's subscription page currently lists $39/month and $179/year, marked down from a former $299 annual list price. Other 2026 sources quote $35/month or $159/year, and pricing varies by region, so check the page you're served.

**Does AlgoMonster replace LeetCode?** No, and it doesn't claim to. It replaces the "which problems should I even be doing" phase with explicit patterns and templates, then leaves you short on volume. 231 problems against LeetCode's 3,000-plus means most people supplement rather than switch.

**Is AlgoMonster cheaper than LeetCode Premium?** On annual billing, yes — Pro Yearly has been listed between $69.99 and $99, and Pro Lifetime between $89 and $189 one-time, both below LeetCode's $179 annual plan. It's a different product, so the comparison is more "map versus gym" than like-for-like.

**Does AlgoMonster have a monthly plan?** No. Annual or lifetime only, with a limited free tier to evaluate the format.

## The short version

LeetCode Premium is a targeting and convenience purchase. It's worth its price when you already solve mediums and you're aiming at named companies inside a defined window — and it's a waste when the actual problem is that you can't yet spot which pattern a problem wants. That second problem is what structured pattern courses exist for, and AlgoMonster's text-first, four-to-eight-week curriculum is the cheapest credible route to fixing it. Buy the map before you buy the gym membership.
